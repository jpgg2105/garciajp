When does LoRA fine-tuning beat a good prompt? I measured the crossover

“Should we fine-tune a small model or prompt a big one?” comes up on every team that builds with LLMs. The honest answer is “it depends on how much labelled data you have”, so I measured how much, on one concrete task.

Short answer: with 100 examples, prompt the big model; with 2,000, the fine-tuned small model catches up. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with 10 retrieved examples got 55% of records exactly right at 100 labelled examples, against 32% for Qwen2.5-1.5B fine-tuned with LoRA on the same 100. At 2,000 examples it was 66% against 64%, a difference within noise, with the fine-tuned model reading 29 times fewer input tokens.

The setup

  • Task: turn a request to a voice assistant into JSON with its intent and slots, using MASSIVE (60 intents, 55 slot types, human labels). “wake me up at five am this week” becomes alarm_set with time “five am” and date “this week”.
  • Same data for every method: pools of 100, 500 and 2,000 labelled examples. LoRA trains on the pool; the prompts get the 10 pool examples most similar to each request, plus the full label lists.
  • Models: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, prompted and fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 16, settings fixed before running) on a free Colab T4; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite through the API, prompted.
  • Scoring: 300 test requests; exact record (intent and every slot right), slot F1, intent accuracy, valid JSON and schema compliance, with paired bootstrap intervals. Three hypotheses were written before the run.

300 test requests. At 2,000 examples, LoRA vs Gemini: −2.3 points [−7.3, +2.7] on exact records, −0.3 on intent, −5.2 [−9.6, −0.8] on slot F1. LoRA at 100 is the mean of three seeds (33, 31, 32).

What the curve says

  • At 100 examples the big model wins by 22 points. It has seen far more language, and its prompt lists every label.
  • On the same small model, fine-tuning beats prompting once there is data: a tie at 100, then +12 points at 500 and +15 at 2,000.
  • The big model's curve flattens: 55%, 64%, 66%. More examples in the pool only change which 10 it sees. The fine-tuned model kept climbing.

Format was never the problem

I expected LoRA to fix formatting first and accuracy later. It didn't need to: every method produced valid JSON on 99.7 to 100% of requests. What failed at 100 examples was labels. The fine-tuned model never sees the label list, and 22 of the 60 intents weren't in its 100 examples, so it invented plausible ones: 58 replies used intents that don't exist, like “recipe” or “email_send”. At 2,000 examples, covering all 60, schema compliance reached 97%.

What it costs

  • Input: 31 tokens per request for the fine-tuned model against about 900 for the prompts with examples.
  • Compute: 190 GPU-seconds per 1,000 requests on a T4, against 355 for the same model prompted.
  • Training: 40 seconds for 100 examples, 3 minutes for 2,000, on a free GPU. The adapter is 1.2% of the model's parameters.

How the hypotheses did

  • With about 100 examples, the prompted big model wins: true, 55% vs 32%.
  • With about 2,000, the fine-tuned model matches it: mostly true. It matched on exact records, intent and valid JSON, and stayed 5 points behind on slot extraction.
  • LoRA fixes format before accuracy: false. Format was fine from the start, and labels were the real problem.

Limits

One task with short, well-defined labels, which suits fine-tuning; open-ended extraction would favour the bigger model more. One seed at 500 and 2,000, LoRA settings not tuned, and the API model is Flash-Lite, not the largest available. Giving the fine-tuned model the label list, or constraining its output to the schema, would probably fix most of its early errors; that's the next experiment, not a result. Every prediction and the code are in lora-vs-prompting on GitHub; how LoRA itself works is in the explainer How fine-tuning with LoRA works.

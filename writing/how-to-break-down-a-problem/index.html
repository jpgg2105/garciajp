How to break down a big problem

Most problems feel hard because they're vague. Splitting one into parts that don't overlap and add back up to the whole turns 'sales are down' into one specific question you can answer.

  1. 1

    Write the problem as one sentence

    Turn the worry into a statement you can measure: what changed, by how much, since when. A clear sentence already rules out half the guesses.

  2. 2

    Split it into parts that make up the whole

    Pick a split where the parts add or multiply back to the problem and don't overlap. Revenue is customers times what each one spends.

  3. 3

    Keep splitting until each part is measurable

    Go one level deeper wherever a part is still too broad to check. Stop when each part is something you can look up or test.

  4. 4

    Check each part with numbers

    Put a number on every part. Most turn out fine, and the one that explains the change stands out.

  5. 5

    Work on the part that moved

    Follow the path down to that part and ask a specific question about it. Small, clear questions get answered; big, vague ones don't.

In short

  • A good split is MECE, mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: nothing falls through the gaps and nothing is counted twice (Minto, 1987).
  • If one split doesn't help, try another: by step, by type of customer, by place, by time.
  • In the example, new and returning customers were 40% and 60% of the old total, so −3% and −31% combine to −20%. Always check that the parts add back up.
  • It works the same for a bug, a budget, a slow process or a personal goal. The numbers in the animation are illustrative.

References

  1. Minto (1987). The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking. Pearson.
  2. Pólya (1945). How to Solve It. Princeton University Press.

How RAG works

Retrieval-augmented generation lets a language model answer from your own documents instead of from memory. Scroll to follow a question from the documents to a cited answer.

  1. 1

    Split the documents

    Long documents are cut into short chunks, a few paragraphs each, so the system can retrieve just the part that answers a question.

  2. 2

    Turn chunks into vectors

    An embedding model turns each chunk into a list of numbers. Chunks with similar meaning land close together in this space.

  3. 3

    Embed the question

    The question goes through the same embedding model, so it lands in the same space as the chunks.

  4. 4

    Find the nearest chunks

    An approximate nearest-neighbor search returns the chunks closest to the question. It's fast, but closeness in embedding space is only a rough guess at relevance.

  5. 5

    Rerank

    A reranker, usually a cross-encoder, reads the question and each candidate together and scores how well it actually answers. The order changes.

  6. 6

    Answer with sources

    The top chunks go into the prompt. The model answers from them and cites the chunk behind each claim.

In short

  • RAG keeps answers grounded in documents you control, and you can update them without retraining the model (Lewis et al., 2020).
  • Retrieval usually decides answer quality: chunking, the embedding model and reranking tend to matter more than prompt wording.
  • Hybrid search, keyword matching (BM25) plus vectors, catches exact terms like product codes that embeddings miss.
  • Evaluate retrieval and generation separately: was the right chunk retrieved, and did the answer stay faithful to it?

References
